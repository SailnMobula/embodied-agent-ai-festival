## What the X2 Ultra has on board

- **RGB camera**: colour, rich in detail, flat, no distance
- **Depth camera**: every pixel also carries how far away it is
- **Point cloud**: the depth image as a few hundred thousand measured points in 3D

To the robot an image is a grid of numbers. Everything below is about turning that grid into
something a program can act on.

Same image, same person, three different answers. For a wave, only the third one will do.

## YOLO, one look is enough

Older detectors searched. Slide a window across the image, propose a few thousand regions, run the
classifier hundreds of times per frame. Accurate, and far too slow for a robot.

YOLO makes one pass. The image becomes a grid, and every cell predicts at the same time: is an
object centred in me, how big is it, which class is it.

- Dozens of frames per second on hardware that fits inside a robot
- A little less accurate per frame
- An answer in 20 milliseconds beats a perfect answer two seconds late

> **Live demo: switch to Rerun.** `detect.py` draws boxes around everyone in the room. Then
> `segment.py`, same question, pixel-exact masks instead of boxes.

## MediaPipe, from a box to a skeleton

A box cannot tell you a gesture. Joints can. MediaPipe is Google's toolkit of small models built to
run on the device rather than in a data centre.

1. **Detect once**: a light detector finds the person and crops to them
2. **Then track**: a second model predicts **33 landmarks**, each an x/y coordinate with a rough
   depth and a confidence

The next frame reuses the previous skeleton instead of detecting again. Detection is expensive,
tracking is cheap. That is the trick that keeps it realtime.

MediaPipe returns 33 points with coordinates. It has no concept of "standing", "pointing" or
"waving".

## So how do we detect a wave?

We write it ourselves. On top of the skeleton, a wave is geometry over time:

1. Wrist **above the shoulder**, which is two y-coordinates compared
2. Elbow bent, so the arm is raised rather than pointing
3. Wrist **moving side to side** across roughly a second

Three conditions true at once and we call it a wave. No network was trained on waving. A person
decided what a wave looks like as numbers and typed it out.

> **Live demo: wave at the camera.** `pose.py` draws the skeleton and runs exactly that rule. Wave,
> and the status flips to `WAVE`. Same signal the robot's wave-back reacts to.

## Seeing in three dimensions

> **Live demo: the point cloud.** `depth.py` rebuilds the room as a rotatable cloud of coloured
> points. Then `fuse.py`: YOLO finds the person on the colour image, the depth camera says how far
> away they are, and the label floats at that spot in 3D. Colour answers _what_, depth answers
> _where_.

## Where it breaks

- Backlight and glass surfaces wreck depth measurements
- A model only recognises what it was trained on, everything else is invisible rather than unknown
- Landmarks jitter between frames, which is why the wave rule looks at a full second
- Our rule is a guess about people: a slow wave with a straight arm never registers, and widening
  the rule starts catching anyone scratching their head

Station 4 turns this into a skill. Station 5 lets the agent decide when to care about it.
