## What the X2 Ultra has on board

- **RGB cameras** — ordinary colour images. Rich in detail, but flat: they carry no distance.
- **Depth cameras** — every pixel additionally carries "how far away". This is what makes stepping
  over an obstacle possible instead of walking into it.
- **Point clouds** — the depth image turned into a cloud of measured points in 3D space. Not a
  smooth model of the room, just a few hundred thousand dots the sensor was able to hit.

A camera image on its own means nothing to a robot. It is a grid of numbers. Everything that
follows is about turning that grid into something a program can act on.

## Three ways to answer "where?"

Computer vision is not one task. Ask a model where the person is and you get a different kind of
answer depending on what it was built for.

## YOLO — one look is enough

**YOLO** stands for *You Only Look Once*, and the name is the whole idea.

Older detectors worked by searching. They slid a window across the image, or proposed a few
thousand candidate regions, and asked "is it here? is it here? is it here?" — running the
classifier hundreds of times per frame. Accurate, and far too slow for a robot.

YOLO turns detection into a single pass. The image is divided into a grid, and in **one** run
through the network every cell simultaneously predicts: is the centre of an object in me, how big
is its box, and what class is it. One look, all objects at once.

That change is what made realtime detection possible — dozens of frames per second on a machine
you can put inside a robot. Accuracy per frame is somewhat lower than the slow methods, but a
robot that answers in 20 milliseconds beats one that answers perfectly two seconds too late.

> **Live demo — switch to Rerun.** `detect.py` runs YOLO on the RealSense feed: boxes around
> everyone in the room, dozens of frames a second. Then `segment.py` — same idea, but pixel-exact
> masks instead of boxes. Same "where?", two different answers.

## MediaPipe — from a box to a skeleton

A box is not enough to recognise a gesture. For that we need joints, and that is what
**MediaPipe** provides. It is Google's toolkit of small models built to run on-device — phones,
laptops, robots — rather than in a data centre.

Its pose solution works in two stages, and the second stage is the interesting one:

1. **Detect once.** A lightweight detector locates the person in the frame and crops to them.
2. **Then track.** A second model runs on that crop and predicts **33 landmarks** — nose,
   shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles — each as an x/y coordinate plus a rough depth
   and a confidence value.

For the next frame it does not detect again. It reuses the previous skeleton to guess where the
person moved and re-runs only the landmark model. Detection is expensive; tracking is cheap. That
is the trick that keeps it realtime.

The output is worth being precise about. MediaPipe returns **33 points with coordinates**. It does
not return "standing", "pointing" or "waving". It has no concept of those things.

## So how do we detect a wave?

We write it ourselves. That is the honest answer, and it is the point of this station.

Given the skeleton, "waving" becomes plain geometry over time:

1. Is the wrist **above the shoulder**? Compare two y-coordinates.
2. Is the elbow bent, so the arm is raised rather than pointing?
3. Does the wrist **move side to side** across roughly a second — left, right, left again?

Three conditions, all true at once, and we call it a wave. No neural network was trained on waving.
No model was asked "is this person waving". A person looked at what MediaPipe outputs, decided what
a wave looks like as numbers, and typed it out.

This is exactly the pattern from Station 1, one level down. The model supplies raw perception; a
human supplies the meaning. In Station 4 this becomes a **skill** the robot can offer, and in
Station 5 the agent gets to decide *when* to care about it — but nobody, at any point, taught an AI
what waving is.

> **Live demo — wave at the camera.** `pose.py` draws the skeleton and runs exactly the rule
> above. Wave, and the status flips to `WAVE`. This is the same signal the robot's wave-back
> reacts to.

## Seeing in three dimensions

The demos so far all drew on the flat colour image. But the depth camera turns every pixel into a
point in space, and that is closer to how the robot actually perceives a room.

> **Live demo — the point cloud.** `depth.py` rebuilds the room as a rotatable cloud of coloured
> points. Then `fuse.py` puts the two together: YOLO finds a person on the colour image, the depth
> camera says how far away they are, and a label floats in the 3D cloud at exactly their position.
> Colour answers *what*, depth answers *where* — fused into one picture.

## Where it breaks

Worth saying out loud during the demo, because it is the honest part:

- Backlight and glass surfaces wreck depth measurements.
- A model only recognises what it was trained on. Everything else is invisible to it, rather than
  "unknown".
- Landmarks jitter between frames. Every rule built on top has to tolerate that — which is why the
  wave check looks at a full second instead of a single frame.
- Our wave rule is a guess about human behaviour. Someone waving slowly, with a straight arm, or
  holding a bag, will not register. Widening the rule to catch them starts catching people who are
  just scratching their head.
